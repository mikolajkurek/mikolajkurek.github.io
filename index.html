    The problem

    Skin brachytherapy usually relies on thermoplastic meshes shaped by hand and layered bolus. Catheter guides shift slightly, air gaps stay between applicator and skin, full-face masks cause anxiety and movement, and every case takes repeated time from physicists and technicians.

    What I built

    2. Reconstruction. Hounsfield-scale segmentation, mesh generation and STL export.
    3. Modelling. Catheter channels at a fixed distance from the skin and shrinkage compensation, automated with my Blender Python add-on.
    4. Planning. Imported into the treatment planning system to simulate the dose.

    6. Clinical QA. Fit verified in the treatment room before the session.

    Clinically confirmed

    Four body regions so far: a distal phalanx (3 channels), the right ear (4), the left ear (3) and the scalp (5, open frame). Anatomical reference points give the same placement in every session, and open-frame designs keep ventilation and field of view. From CT scan to finished applicator takes under a week.

    Result

    96% correct on the real belt, 46 ms per frame.82 of 85 random trials sorted correctly, mAP@0.5 of 0.995 on validation, and the whole model runs on the camera chip at about 30 frames per second, with the Raspberry Pi CPU left free.

    In numbers

    Trials

    Model
    YOLOv8n, 3.2M parameters, FP32 to INT8, 7.06 of 8 MB on the sensor (89%)
    Speed

    Data

    Accuracy
    mAP@0.5 0.995, every class on the diagonal of the confusion matrix
    Endurance

    The problem

    Quality control on small production lines usually means PLCs and industrial cameras: expensive, and hard to reconfigure. My thesis asked whether one person could build a sorter from consumer parts that recognises products by how they look, and can be taught new ones in software instead of rebuilding the machine. The test case: five tea flavours, plus damaged and misplaced boxes.

    What I built

    1. Mechanics. A T-slot conveyor with every custom part designed in Fusion 360, printed in PETG and assembled by me.
    2. Electronics. Schematic and PCB designed in KiCad, prototyped on a breadboard, then soldered and calibrated.
    3. Data. My own photo dataset, labelled box by box, balanced and augmented twelve-fold.
    4. Model. YOLOv8n trained with transfer learning on a T4 GPU.
    5. Edge AI. Quantised to INT8 and packed into the 8 MB memory of the Sony IMX500 sensor.
    6. Software. Multithreaded Python control: detection, belt motor, decisions and signal lights.
    7. Interface. An operator dashboard designed in Figma for live monitoring.

    Edge AI: a detector in 8 MB

    The Raspberry Pi AI Camera runs the network on the image sensor itself, so the Pi only receives finished detections, never raw frames to process. The catch is memory: 8 MB for weights, firmware and working buffers together. Of the YOLOv8 family only the Nano version fits; Small already needs 21 MB.

    1. Training. Transfer learning from COCO, 640 by 640 input, cosine learning rate, mixed precision. Early stopping ended the run at epoch 75, with the best weights from around epoch 25.
    2. Quantisation. Post-training quantisation from 32-bit floats to 8-bit integers, calibrated on my own images so each layer keeps its range and the accuracy survives.
    3. Fit check. The memory report confirmed 7.06 MB in use, 89% of the chip, before I packed it into the firmware file the camera loads at start.
    4. Workaround. Sony's export tool lost track of file paths and failed; I traced it and found a reliable way around it.

    The dataset

    No dataset existed for this, so I made one: 186 photos, labelled by hand in Label Studio, split 80 to 20 for training and validation. Damaged boxes are rare on a real line, so a model trained on natural data would just call everything fine. I damaged boxes on purpose to keep the classes close to 50 to 50.

    Each training photo became 12 variants in Albumentations: brightness, gamma and colour shifts for changing light, flips, rotation, scale and shear for any position on the belt, and noise and motion blur for a moving object. The validation set stayed untouched, so the scores are honest.

    Circuit design in KiCad

    A board between the 3.3 V logic of the Pi and the 12 V motor. I drew the schematic and laid out the PCB in KiCad, keeping the logic and power sides apart, then checked the 3D model for collisions before building anything.

    1. Motor driver. DRV8825 with microstepping for a smooth belt, so the camera image stays sharp.
    2. Power cut-off. An N-channel MOSFET switches the motor off completely: about 0 mA when idle.
    3. Protection. A 47 µF capacitor against voltage spikes; 220 Ω resistors keep each LED at a safe 10 to 15 mA for the GPIO.
    4. Calibration. Set the driver's reference voltage to 242 mV with a multimeter, limiting the motor to 0.48 A so it runs cool.

    Mechanics, printed and assembled by me

    The frame is 30x30 aluminium T-slot. Every other part I designed in Fusion 360 and printed in PETG on a Bambu Lab X1C: 0.2 mm layers, 15% gyroid infill and four walls around threads and bearing seats. The rollers turn on 608ZZ bearings; a NEMA 17 stepper drives the belt through a GT2 belt and 40 to 30 tooth pulleys, so the speed is exact without an encoder. Sliding the motor along the profile tensions the belt, no extra tensioner needed.

    Parts I designed, printed and assembled

    Every printed part has its own technical drawing, made in Fusion 360 in December 2025. Click one to open it.

    Control software

    Python 3.11 on Raspberry Pi OS. The camera thread reads detections straight from the sensor's metadata every 0.1 s and signals the main loop; a lock keeps the shared results consistent, so the motor never stutters while the camera works.

    1. Run. Green light, belt moving.
    2. Detect. A box appears: the motor is cut within milliseconds, holding it under the camera.
    3. Decide. The most confident detection wins: yellow for a good box, red for a damaged or misplaced one, then the belt restarts.

    What counts as a defect is a single list in the config, so the sorting rules change without touching the control loop. The confidence threshold is 0.80, just below the F1 peak of 0.84, to favour catching every box in motion.

    Operator dashboard

    A high-fidelity interface designed in Figma for a separate operator terminal: live camera view with detections, an event log with class and confidence, detections per hour and class distribution, and CPU, RAM and uptime of the controller.

    Problems I solved

    1. Glare. Glossy boxes reflected the overhead light and hid the damage; side lighting moved the reflections out of the lens and made dents cast shadows.
    2. Rare defects. Balanced the classes by creating the damage myself instead of waiting for it.
    3. The 3 misses. All were damaged boxes whose torn side was outside the frame. Not a model error: the next step is a guide that centres boxes before the camera.

    07 Design and simulation, FEM and CFD

    Simulation studies in Ansys

    Three studies from my simulation courses: heat through a chimney wall, a water jet hitting a wall and a composite plate loaded to its limit. Each one checked against a balance or a finer mesh, not just trusted.

    Role
    Modelling, meshing, solving and verification
    When
    2025
    Where
    Cracow University of Technology
    Stack
    Ansys FluentAnsys Mechanicalk-epsilonOrthotropic materials

    Result

    Errors below 0.002%.The energy balance closed to 0.0018% in the heat study and the mass balance to 0.00003% in the flow study; refining the mesh cut the remaining temperature error more than tenfold.

    Heat through a chimney

    Steady conduction through a dolomite chimney wall in Fluent. The cross-section is symmetric, so I modelled a quarter of it with symmetry planes. A shell mesh with ideal skewness and orthogonal quality converged in about ten iterations; the energy balance closed to 0.0018%, and refining the mesh from 0.5 to 0.25 mm brought the error at the check points from 0.31% to 0.03%.

    Water jet against a wall

    A water jet leaving a pipe at 4 m/s and hitting a wall, solved as turbulent flow with the k-epsilon model. The default mesher left an irregular patch, so I switched to PrimeMesh quadrilaterals (10,162 elements). With the absolute criteria off the solution settled after about 800 iterations; the mass balance closed to 0.00003% and the jet pushes on the wall with about 0.05 N.

    Composite plate to its limit

    A plate fixed on one edge and loaded on the other, run in Ansys Mechanical as structural steel, zinc and an orthotropic fibre composite. I compared deformation, equivalent and normal stress and the safety factor, then raised the load until the safety factor fell below 1, at about 107 kN.

    09 Software and AI, data analysis

    EV fleet efficiency analysis

    How much does winter cost an electric fleet? Three years of Chicago fleet and weather data, modelled in Power BI and tested with ANOVA.

    Role
    Data cleaning, modelling, DAX, dashboards and statistics
    When
    2026
    Data
    Chicago, IL, 2020 to 2023: 1,516 records after ETL
    Stack
    Power BIDAXPower QueryjamoviANOVA

    Result

    Winter costs 53% more energy.3.58 kWh per mile in winter against 2.34 in autumn. Welch ANOVA, F(3, 742) = 20.2, p < 0.001: winter differs from every other season.

    The problem

    Fleet budgets often treat every month the same. The questions: does weather change how efficiently electric buses and yard tractors use their batteries, and is it the cold or the snow that matters?

    What I built

    1. ETL. Cleaned and joined trip, energy and weather data in Power Query into 1,516 records.
    2. Model. A star schema: trips and weather linked one-to-many through a shared calendar dimension.
    3. Measures. Nine DAX measures, including year-to-date and year-on-year with TOTALYTD and SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR.
    4. Dashboards. Three report pages: fleet overview by season, weather against efficiency, and the trend over time.
    5. Statistics. One-way ANOVA in jamovi. Levene and Shapiro-Wilk ruled out the classic test, so I used Welch, then Tukey post-hoc.

    What the data says

    1. Temperature, not snow. Efficiency falls from about 4 kWh per mile at −20 °C to 2.2 at +28 °C, while speed is the same in all three snow categories: it is battery chemistry, not road conditions.
    2. Winter stands apart. Tukey post-hoc: winter differs from spring, summer and autumn, by up to 1.23 kWh per mile.
    3. It holds up. The effect stays with classic and Welch ANOVA, and for buses alone (n = 689, p = 0.003).
    4. What to do. Budget 30 to 50% more energy for winter, and watch it on the dashboard as it happens.

    11 Software and AI, data science

    ECG anomaly detection

    A 1D convolutional network that flags arrhythmias in single heartbeats, tuned so that critical cases are not missed, with a small web app to try it on real recordings.

    Data
    MIT-BIH arrhythmia dataset: 87,553 heartbeats, 187 samples each at 125 Hz
    When
    2024
    Stack
    PythonTensorFlowKerasPandasNumPyscikit-learnMatplotlibFlask

    Notebook ↗︎

    Result

    97.6% of arrhythmias caught.Only 70 of 2,934 abnormal beats missed in the test set, with 91% accuracy overall. Tuned for sensitivity on purpose, because in a clinic a missed anomaly costs far more than a false alarm.

    The problem

    In MIT-BIH about 83% of heartbeats are normal and the rarest arrhythmia class is under 1%, and no new samples could be added. A naive model learns to call everything normal and still scores well on accuracy.

    The data

    Each sample is one heartbeat cut to 187 points at 125 Hz. Arrhythmias come in four kinds, and some look very close to a normal beat.

    What I built

    1. Preparation. Merged five beat classes into normal and abnormal, scaled the signals and split train, validation and test.
    2. Model. A compact 1D CNN in Keras: two convolution and pooling stages, dropout against overfitting, a sigmoid output.
    3. Imbalance. Class weights that make each missed arrhythmia cost about 14 times more than a false alarm.
    4. Evaluation. Recall as the main metric, with the decision threshold lowered to 0.3; the trade-off is more false alarms that a doctor can rule out.
    5. App. A Flask web app that plots a recording and returns the prediction with its confidence, filterable by normal and abnormal samples.

    Try it

    The Flask app loads a balanced sample of 100 beats, plots each one and shows the prediction next to the true label. On that sample the model gets 92 of 100 right.
